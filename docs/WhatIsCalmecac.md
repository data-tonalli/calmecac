# The Calmecac

![calmecac](../calmecac/.claude/skills/plan/calmecac.png)

In the heart of Tenochtitlan stood the Calmecac — the Aztec institution of higher learning where future leaders were forged through rigorous intellectual formation. You entered the Calmecac not to memorize facts, but to learn how to *think systematically*. Only after that formation were you ready to act.

This skill carries that name because it does the same thing for ideas. A half-baked idea enters the Calmecac. Six teachers — drawn from six civilizations — each take a turn guiding the student: questioning the idea, structuring it into an architecture, attacking it to find weaknesses, rebuilding it stronger, specifying its exact behavioral requirements, and finally decomposing it into the smallest possible implementable steps. What comes out is not code — it is a complete, traceable, battle-tested plan of action ready for a coding loop to execute.

---

## The Vision

Imagine a Calmecac outside of time — a house of learning where teachers from different civilizations, separated by centuries and continents, gather around a single student. Each brings the methodology of their tradition. They do not compete; they build on each other's work. The student's idea passes through each teacher's hands, growing stronger and more precise at every step.

This is that Calmecac.

---

## How It Works

### `just plan` — Plan

An interactive session where six teachers — drawn from six civilizations — guide you through structured thinking:

1. **Socrates** asks questions to clarify what you want to build
2. **Khayyam** designs the architecture
3. **Popper** attacks the plan to find weaknesses
4. **Zhu Xi** rebuilds the plan stronger
5. **Euclid** writes precise requirements and test specs
6. **Al-Khwarizmi** breaks everything into atomic tasks

You review and approve each phase. The output is a complete workspace with specs, requirements, tests, and an ordered task list.

### `just build` — Build

**Vera** reads the workspace the plan produced and builds the project autonomously. One task at a time, tested, committed. It loops until everything is done or it gets stuck.

## Biographies

**Socrates** (c. 470–399 BCE) discovers what you actually mean.

Socrates never wrote a word. Everything we know about him comes through others — mainly Plato — because his method was entirely oral: relentless, patient questioning that forced his interlocutors to examine their own assumptions. He believed that no one could build something sound on ideas they hadn't first tested against contradiction. The Athenian courts eventually sentenced him to death for this habit of making powerful people realize they didn't know what they thought they knew. He is named a teacher here because his method — the elenchus — is the original tool for turning a vague idea into something precise enough to build.

**Omar Khayyam** (1048–1131) gives the idea structure.

Khayyam was a Persian polymath — mathematician, astronomer, and poet — who solved cubic equations geometrically three centuries before European algebra caught up. He led the team that reformed the Persian calendar into one more accurate than the Gregorian calendar that replaced it. His Rubaiyat, later made famous in English by Edward FitzGerald, is often misread as hedonism; it is closer to a mathematician's meditation on what can and cannot be known. He is named a teacher here because he could look at a tangled problem and find the structure hiding inside it — the skeleton that holds everything else together.

**Karl Popper** (1902–1994) tries to break what was just built.

Popper was an Austrian-British philosopher who argued that the mark of a scientific theory is not that it can be proven true, but that it can be proven false. He fled Vienna ahead of the Nazis and spent his career in London dismantling the idea that knowledge advances by confirmation. Verification is easy and seductive; falsification is hard and useful. He is named a teacher here because plans that survive only friendly scrutiny are plans that fail in production. His role is to attack the design before reality does.

**Zhu Xi** (1130–1200) rebuilds the plan stronger.

Zhu Xi was a Song Dynasty scholar who synthesized centuries of fragmented Confucian thought into a coherent system — the Neo-Confucian framework that shaped East Asian intellectual life for seven hundred years. His method was *gewu zhizhi*: investigate things to extend knowledge. He believed understanding came not from isolated insights but from patiently fitting observations into a unified whole. He is named a teacher here because after Popper tears the plan apart, someone must gather what survives and rebuild it into something more resilient than what came before.

**Euclid** (fl. c. 300 BCE) writes down exactly what must be true.

Euclid compiled the *Elements*, thirteen books that organized all known geometry into a chain of propositions, each one following inevitably from the ones before it. Starting from five postulates and five common notions, he derived 465 theorems — a structure so rigorous it remained the standard textbook for over two thousand years. Almost nothing is known about his life; the work is the biography. He is named a teacher here because once the plan is rebuilt, its requirements must be stated with the kind of precision that leaves no room for ambiguity — axioms, not wishes.

**Al-Khwarizmi** (c. 780–850) turns the spec into a sequence of steps.

Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi was a mathematician at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad whose book *Al-Kitab al-Mukhtasar fi Hisab al-Jabr wal-Muqabala* gave algebra its name and whose methods gave us the word *algorithm*. He didn't just solve equations — he wrote systematic, repeatable procedures that anyone could follow to arrive at the correct answer. He is named a teacher here because the final act of planning is to take everything the other teachers produced and decompose it into an ordered sequence of atomic tasks — small enough to execute, clear enough to verify, ordered so each one builds on the last.

**Vera Rubin** (1928–2016) executes the plan.

Vera Rubin was an American astronomer whose meticulous observation of galaxy rotation curves provided the strongest evidence that the universe is dominated by dark matter — mass that is real but invisible. Despite facing systemic barriers throughout her career (she was denied access to observatories, excluded from talks, told astronomy wasn't for women), she kept observing, kept measuring, and kept following the evidence wherever it led. She is named a builder here because she embodies what it means to do the work: systematic, evidence-driven, thorough, and relentless.

## The Cultural Mosaic

Six teachers from six civilizations — Greek, Persian, Austrian, Chinese, Alexandrian, and Arab — all gathered in a Mesoamerican house of learning. This reflects the creator's Mexica heritage and the belief that the best thinking draws from all traditions. No single culture has a monopoly on wisdom. The Calmecac is strongest when every tradition contributes its sharpest tool.

Welcome to the Calmecac. Bring your idea. The teachers are waiting.
