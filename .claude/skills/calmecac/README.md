# The Calmecac

![calmecac](calmecac.png)

In the heart of Tenochtitlan stood the Calmecac — the Aztec institution of higher learning where future leaders were forged through rigorous intellectual formation. You entered the Calmecac not to memorize facts, but to learn how to *think systematically*. Only after that formation were you ready to act.

This skill carries that name because it does the same thing for ideas. A half-baked idea enters the Calmecac. Six teachers — drawn from six civilizations — each take a turn guiding the student: questioning the idea, structuring it into an architecture, attacking it to find weaknesses, rebuilding it stronger, specifying its exact behavioral requirements, and finally decomposing it into the smallest possible implementable steps. What comes out is not code — it is a complete, traceable, battle-tested plan of action ready for a coding loop to execute.

---

## The Vision

Imagine a Calmecac outside of time — a house of learning where teachers from different civilizations, separated by centuries and continents, gather around a single student. Each brings the methodology of their tradition. They do not compete; they build on each other's work. The student's idea passes through each teacher's hands, growing stronger and more precise at every step.

This is that Calmecac.

## The Teachers

### Socrates — Discovery
**Greece, ~470–399 BCE**

Socrates never wrote a word. He taught by asking questions — relentlessly, precisely, until his students discovered what they actually believed and where their reasoning broke down. The Socratic method strips away assumptions and exposes contradictions. It does not add information; it removes confusion.

He opens the Calmecac because discovery is the foundation. Before you can plan, build, or test, you must know what you are actually trying to do. Socrates ensures that no idea passes through without being questioned to its core.

### Omar Khayyam — Construction
**Persia, 1048–1131 CE**

Poet, astronomer, and mathematician. Khayyam systematized the solution of cubic equations — not by brute force, but by classifying them into structured categories and solving each category with its own method. He reformed the Persian calendar with a precision that surpassed the Gregorian calendar by centuries.

He builds the architectural plan because construction is the art of decomposing complex problems into structured, solvable categories. Where Socrates asked "what?", Khayyam asks "how does this fit together?"

### Karl Popper — Destruction
**Austria, 1902–1994 CE**

Popper overturned the prevailing philosophy of science with a single insight: you cannot prove a theory true by finding evidence that supports it — you can only strengthen it by failing to prove it false. A theory that cannot be attacked cannot be trusted. Strength comes from surviving genuine attempts to break, not from confirmation.

He attacks the plan because the most dangerous architectures are the ones no one tried to break. Popper does not build — he searches for the scenario where everything falls apart. What survives his scrutiny is genuinely strong.

### Zhu Xi — Reconciliation
**China, 1130–1200 CE**

Zhu Xi inherited a fractured intellectual landscape — Confucianism, Buddhism, and Daoism competing for authority, each with genuine insights and genuine blind spots. Rather than picking a winner, he synthesized them into Neo-Confucianism, a unified framework that was richer than any single tradition. His synthesis shaped Chinese thought for seven centuries.

He reconciles the plan with its critiques because synthesis is about forging something stronger from competing ideas, not picking a winner. Zhu Xi reads Khayyam's architecture and Popper's attacks, and produces a new plan that is better than either alone.

### Euclid of Alexandria — Specification
**Alexandria, ~300 BCE**

Before Euclid, geometry was a scattered collection of known truths — useful, but disorganized and incomplete. Euclid's *Elements* turned that scattered knowledge into a precise, traceable chain of propositions: definitions, axioms, theorems, proofs. Each statement followed from what came before. Nothing was assumed without being stated.

He extracts requirements because specification is turning informal understanding into structured, verifiable contracts. Every behavior becomes a proposition. Every proposition traces to an invariant. Nothing is implicit.

### Al-Khwarizmi — Decomposition
**Baghdad, ~780–850 CE**

His name gave us the word "algorithm." Working in the House of Wisdom, Al-Khwarizmi formalized the art of breaking solutions into explicit, sequential, unambiguous steps. His methods were so clear that a person unfamiliar with the problem could follow them mechanically and arrive at the correct answer.

He closes the Calmecac because task decomposition is exactly that — turning requirements into a sequence of atomic operations that a coding agent can follow mechanically. Each task does one thing. Each task is verifiable. Execute them top to bottom and the system is built.

## The Cultural Mosaic

Six teachers from six civilizations — Greek, Persian, Austrian, Chinese, Alexandrian, and Arab — all gathered in a Mesoamerican house of learning. This reflects the creator's Mexica heritage and the belief that the best thinking draws from all traditions. No single culture has a monopoly on wisdom. The Calmecac is strongest when every tradition contributes its sharpest tool.

Welcome to the Calmecac. Bring your idea. The teachers are waiting.
